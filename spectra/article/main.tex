\begin{abstract}
Every frontier language model writes with an accent.
Measuring \nStyleTraits{} style traits computed from the text and \nBehaviorTraits{} behaviors labeled by two coder models, for \nModels{} models on \nPrompts{} everyday prompts, we find that which model wrote an answer explains a median \accentMedModel{} of the variation between single answers, against \accentMedPrompt{} for the prompt, yet each model's averages keep their order across tasks, across runs, and on \nPrompts{} new prompts locked before collection.
With three answers per prompt, a larger part appears: each model reacts to particular prompts in its own repeatable way, \reactRatioLow{} to \reactRatio{} times its steady accent, and generic habits such as warm openers barely differ between models overall but appear on different prompts for each.
The accent lives in punctuation and word choice.
The models mishear it.
Their descriptions of themselves and each other match the measurements only weakly (rank correlations of \descRankLow{} to \descRankHigh{}) and overstate generic AI habits, such as caveats \descCaveat{}-fold; their self-portraits let a reader recognize them \portraitSelf{} of the time, against \portraitBlind{} blind and \portraitMeasured{} with measured profiles; writing as another model, they move only \imName{} of the way toward it; asked to hide, they are still named \hideUncoached{} of the time.
Measurement makes the accent usable: a measured profile moves imitations \imProfile{} of the way, and coaching on measured habits cuts identification to \hideCoached{}.
Accents are also contagious: after reading another model's notes, answers move \conShift{} of the way toward its accent; in a style swap that rewrites the notes to keep their content, most of that is what the notes say, and their voice moves answers \swapVoice{} of the way, though rewriting cannot hold the content exactly fixed.
Our later experiments were pre-registered, including a test in which two models from two more vendors joined the panel; the accent, the misheard descriptions and contagion held.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

People who use language models daily notice how differently they write: one answers in two lines and another in two screens; one opens with the answer and another with a compliment; one is fond of em dashes and its sibling from the same vendor almost never uses them.
These habits matter.
Length and formatting shift what both human and model judges prefer \citep{zheng2023judging,dubois2024lengthcontrolled,lmsys2024stylecontrol}, word choices mark model-written text at scale \citep{kobak2024delving}, and models can be told apart from their writing with high accuracy \citep{sun2025idiosyncrasies}.
Corpus linguists now describe the output of each model as an idiolect \citep{reinhart2025writelikehumans,rudnicka2026idiolect}.

We call this voice an \emph{accent}, and ask three questions about it.
\emph{Is it stable?} If a habit depends mostly on the prompt, it is not much of a voice.
\emph{Can the models hear it?} A model that knows its own accent could describe it, imitate another's, or hide its own.
\emph{Does it spread?} Agents now read each other's output as notes, memory and retrieved context; if accents travel through that text, voices in multi-agent systems will blend.

The answers are yes, barely, and yes.
Spectra measures \nStyleTraits{} style traits computed from the text and \nBehaviorTraits{} behaviors labeled by two coder models outside the panel, on \nPrompts{} everyday prompts.
Per answer, the prompt dominates: it explains a median \accentMedPrompt{} of the variation in a trait, and the model \accentMedModel{}.
But the model's share is a steady shift, not noise: the order of the models holds in every task category, in a second run, and on \nPrompts{} new prompts that were locked before any answer to them existed.
A small accent, consistently applied, is enough for a classifier, and for a model given a description, to name the author of most answers.
Repeated answers show that the steady shift is only part of a model's voice: each model also reacts to particular prompts in its own repeatable way, and with these reactions, which model wrote an answer accounts for a median \vcModelReact{} of a trait's variance in a single answer.

The models barely know their accents.
They describe themselves and each other with the stereotype of AI writing, not with the habits that distinguish them.
Portraits they write of themselves barely help a reader; asked to write as another model, they barely move; asked to hide, they stay recognizable.
Measurement changes that: given a measured profile, a model imitates another far better and hides itself far better, and a profile built on one set of prompts lets models name each other's answers on another.
Finally, a model that reads another model's earlier answers as notes picks up part of its accent: mostly through what the notes say, but also, in a style swap designed to keep the content, through their voice.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The accent, measured.} An instrument without quality judges (style computed from the text, behaviors labeled by two coder models outside the panel) and a decomposition of each habit into what the model and the prompt contribute, with consistency across categories, across runs, and on a locked second prompt set (Section~\ref{sec:accent}); with repeated answers, a model's steady accent separated from its reactions to particular prompts and from noise (Section~\ref{sec:reactions}).
\item \textbf{Models mishear accents.} Four pre-registered tests: describing, recognizing from self-written portraits, imitating from a name, and hiding (Section~\ref{sec:hearing}).
\item \textbf{Measurement makes accents usable.} Measured profiles transfer to unseen prompts for identification, and make imitation and hiding work (Section~\ref{sec:usable}).
\item \textbf{Accents are contagious.} The first measurement, to our knowledge, of one model's accent moving into another's answers through shared context, with a style swap that separates the voice of the notes from their content as far as rewriting allows (Section~\ref{sec:contagion}).
\item \textbf{Two more models.} With two models from two more vendors in the panel, five registered tests hold: the accent's size and order, the newcomers' own stable habits, the stereotyped descriptions, and contagion to new readers; readers also differ widely in how far notes move them (Section~\ref{sec:eight}).
\item \textbf{Open and pre-registered.} The pipeline, answers, labels and experiments, with pre-registrations committed before their data, including one design flaw we found afterwards (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Model voices.}
Models write differently from humans and from each other in grammar and rhetoric \citep{reinhart2025writelikehumans}, along Biber's dimensions \citep{milicka2026stylisticvariation}, and in individual, idiolect-like patterns that shift between model generations \citep{rudnicka2026idiolect}; style words mark their text in the scientific literature \citep{kobak2024delving}, and \citet{shaib2025slop} give interpretable dimensions for low-quality model text.
Spectra adds a decomposition of each habit into model and prompt, tests its consistency where the prompt changes, and measures what models know about it.

\paragraph{Telling models apart.}
Attributing generated text began with naming which of $k$ generators wrote a passage \citep{uchendu2020attribution,uchendu2021turingbench,huang2024attributionsurvey}.
A fine-tuned embedding classifier names the source among five chat model families with 97.1\% accuracy, and the signal survives rewriting \citep{sun2025idiosyncrasies}.
VibeCheck \citep{dunlap2025vibecheck} scores qualitative differences with judges; LLMmap \citep{pasquini2025llmmap} and Hide and Seek \citep{iourovitski2024hideseek} identify models with crafted queries, and \citet{ko2026whosbehind} find that attribution degrades when domain and version shift.
Peer evaluation measures identity and position effects when models grade each other \citep{margalit2026agentsaspeers}.
We use identification as a check on descriptions, not as a goal.

\paragraph{What models know about themselves.}
Personality self-assessments of models shift with wording and option order \citep{gupta2024selfassessment}, though some are reliable under controlled prompting \citep{serapio2023personality,lee2025trait}.
After fine-tuning, models predict their own behavior better than other models trained on it \citep{binder2024lookinginward}, and describe behaviors they were trained into \citep{betley2025tellme}.
Model judges recognize and favor their own generations \citep{panickssery2024selfrecognition}, sometimes without consistent reasons \citep{ackerman2024recognize}, recognize better between two texts than from one \citep{zhou2025implicitexplicit}, or not at all, once style is controlled \citep{davidson2024selfrecognition,barkhordar2026stylenotself}; self-preference tracks familiarity \citep{wataoka2024selfpreference,goel2025thinkalike}.
We test descriptions against measured habits, with every model describing every other as a control, without fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Imitating style.}
Models imitate formal human writing reasonably and casual writing poorly from a few samples \citep{wang2025catchme}, and prompting strategy matters more than model size \citep{jemama2025imitate}.
We found no study of one model imitating another's voice.

\paragraph{Accommodation and context.}
Models converge toward the style of the conversation they continue, more than humans do \citep{blevins2026accommodate,blevins2026bothways}; humans drifting toward model style is modeled by \citet{thejaswi2026monoculture}.
Narrow in-context examples change model behavior on unrelated tasks \citep{afonin2025emergent}, and instructions drift over a dialogue \citep{li2024instability}.
Contagion between models, through notes rather than dialogue, is to our knowledge unmeasured.

\paragraph{Style in evaluation, and the traps.}
Verbosity and position biases of model judges \citep{zheng2023judging}, length control \citep{dubois2024lengthcontrolled} and style control \citep{lmsys2024stylecontrol} motivate measuring style directly; YapBench measures length alone \citep{borisov2026yapbench}.
Our traps follow dedicated benchmarks for sycophancy \citep{sharma2024sycophancy}, false premises \citep{hu2023falseqa}, ambiguity \citep{zhang2024clamber}, verifiable instructions \citep{zhou2023ifeval} and hedging \citep{zhou2024unreliable}, embedded in everyday prompts.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Prompts and models}
The prompt set has \nPrompts{} prompts, \nPerCategory{} in each of \nCategories{} categories: writing, editing, information, explanation, how-to, advice, coding, summarizing, brainstorming and opinion.
Of these, \nTrapPrompts{} carry a trap: a false premise (\trap{fp}, \nFP{} prompts), pasted text with an error (\trap{err}, \nErr{}), an underspecified request (\trap{amb}, \nAmb{}), a stated opinion (\trap{op}, \nOp{}), or emotional framing (\trap{emo}, \nEmo{}); \nLimitPrompts{} state a limit that a program checks, of \nLimitKinds{} kinds.
A second set of \nPrompts{} prompts mirrors the first slot for slot (category, trap and limit) with new content; it was locked by hash before any answer to it was collected.
We measure \nModels{} models from four providers (Table~\ref{tab:settings}), each answering every prompt once per run (three times in one collection, Section~\ref{sec:accent-method}), with no system prompt, through an API, with output capped at \maxTokens{} tokens (\maxTokensArgon{} for \longModel{}).

\subsection{Measuring habits}
\label{sec:building}
\paragraph{Computed style.}
Style traits are computed from an answer's prose, fenced code set aside (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:traits}): length (geometric mean of words), formatting marks, hedges, caveats, warm openers, closing offers, exclamations and emoji, em dashes, stock phrases, reading ease \citep{flesch1948readability}, vocabulary range \citep{covington2010mattr}, self-reference and limit adherence.
Rates are per 100 words, with a floor of 50 words in the denominator.
\paragraph{Coded behavior.}
Two coder models outside the panel, \coderA{} and \coderB{}, label every answer at temperature 0 on a fixed list (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:codebook}), never seeing the author; a behavior trait is the share of positive labels averaged over the coders, and a trap label applies only to prompts with that trap.
\paragraph{Statistics.}
Answers are averaged within a prompt, then across prompts, with 95\% intervals from 2{,}000 resamples of prompts.
The effect size $d$ is a model's mean minus the panel mean over the pooled within-model standard deviation.
Signature words come from log-odds with an informative Dirichlet prior \citep{monroe2008fightin}.

\subsection{The accent}
\label{sec:accent-method}
For each everyday trait (all but the traps and limit adherence) we split the variation between single answers, on prompts every model answered, into the part explained by which model wrote the answer and the part explained by which prompt it answers (two-way main effects); the rest is a model's reaction to a particular prompt plus noise.
To separate those two, every model answered each prompt of the main run twice more (pre-registered), and a two-way analysis of variance with replication splits each of the \repNTraits{} style traits into the model, the prompt, the model's \emph{reaction} to the prompt (the model-by-prompt interaction) and noise.
An $F$ test compares the reactions with the noise (pre-registered).
Since sums of squares hand a third of the noise to the interaction when there are three answers, we also report each part's share of a single answer's variance, estimated from expected mean squares, with intervals from \bootReps{} resamples of prompts and three robustness checks (post hoc; formulas in Appendix~\ref{app:variance}).
A model's \emph{self-consistency} is the share of its own variation that lies between prompts rather than between repeat answers to one prompt.
Kendall's $W$ measures whether the \nCategories{} categories rank the models alike (0 none, 1 perfect), and a Spearman correlation whether the order of the model means survives into another run or prompt set.
A model's \emph{accent card} lists its departures of $|d|\ge 0.2$ that hold on the same side of the panel in at least 80\% of categories.
Distances between models over the everyday traits' effect sizes, joined by average linkage, give a family tree.

\subsection{Identification}
\label{sec:game-method}
In the guessing game, a model receives a prompt and the \nModels{} unsigned answers, labeled with letters shuffled for each guesser, and names the author of each; it is not told which model it is, and its own answer is scored apart, so headline accuracy measures knowledge of others (chance \chance).
It plays \emph{blind}, with a \emph{profile from the same answers} (a plain-text brief of the measurements of the whole run, the upper reference), and with a \emph{profile from other prompts}: the prompts are split into halves stratified by category, the measurements are re-run on each half, and each half is guessed with the other's profile.
Under the default rule each name is used once; we also play the held-out profile with names allowed to repeat.
A majority and a skill-weighted vote of the other five pool the guessers.
As references, logistic-regression author classifiers \citep{pedregosa2011sklearn} are trained on prompts they are not tested on, from word n-grams and style features or from character 2--5-grams, scored one answer at a time and with a prompt's six answers named jointly; we also retrain the character classifier on text with layers removed and on fewer answers per model.

\subsection{Can models hear accents?}
\label{sec:hearing-method}
\paragraph{Describe.}
Each model describes its own writing and every other model's: a written portrait and numeric estimates of \nEstimates{} traits we measure, scored by rank against the measurements; every model is described by five others, which controls for describing oneself.
Five samples were collected, the later ones with the panel and estimates listed in a shuffled order.
\paragraph{Recognize from portraits.}
The strongest guesser, \texttt{gemini-4-argon}, plays the game with its brief replaced by the portraits: each model's portrait of itself, or of it by another model (never argon), and is compared with its own blind and measured-profile games on the same prompts.
\paragraph{Imitate.}
On 30 everyday prompts, each model answers ``the way \emph{X} would answer it'' for each other model \emph{X}, from the name alone and with \emph{X}'s measured portrait appended, and once with its own name as \emph{X}.
We measure how far an imitation's trait vector moves from the imitator's earlier answer to the same prompt toward the target's answer (0 stays, 1 arrives).
\paragraph{Hide.}
On 50 everyday prompts, each model rewrites its own earlier answer ``so that a reader could not tell which AI model wrote it'', uncoached and coached with its measured habits; a character classifier trained on other prompts tries to name the author.

\subsection{Contagion}
\label{sec:contagion-method}
On 40 everyday prompts, each model answers after reading five earlier answers to other prompts, presented as notes from earlier work: its own five, or the same five prompts answered by one of three models with distinct accents (\conSources), or no notes.
Nothing asks for imitation.
Comparing answers after another model's notes with answers after one's own holds the framing and the note prompts fixed, though not what the notes say; the shift is projected on the axis from the reader's accent to the source's.
\paragraph{The style swap.}
To separate the voice of the notes from their content (pre-registered), each set of notes is first rewritten ``in your own words and your own usual style, keeping all of its content and facts'', either by its author or by the other model.
This crosses whose content the notes carry, the reader's or the source's, with whose voice they are in, and every note set has passed through the same rewriting step.
The \emph{voice effect} is the shift toward the source when the notes are in its voice rather than the reader's, comparing note sets meant to carry the same content and averaged over both contents; the \emph{content effect} is the converse.
In the voice effect a rewrite by the other model enters once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign, so a loss of content common to all such rewrites cancels; content a rewriter adds from its own answers does not cancel, and would inflate the voice effect.
A manipulation check asks whether a classifier names the rewriter of a rewritten note more often than its original author.

\subsection{Pre-registration}
\label{sec:prereg}
Everything after the first two runs and the first guessing games was pre-registered: hypotheses, metrics, prompt lists, message texts and code were committed and pushed before the first call, with deviations appended afterwards.
The second prompt set was locked by SHA-256 (\texttt{\vTwoHash}\dots) before collection and used once.
The registrations and their results files are in the public repository (\repoLink, directory \texttt{spectra/prereg/}); Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prereg} gives the commit that added each one and the time of the first record it governs.

\paragraph{Small per answer.}
The prompt sets most of what an answer looks like (Figure~\ref{fig:accent}).
Across \accentNTraits{} everyday traits, the prompt explains a median \accentMedPrompt{} of the variation between single answers and the model a median \accentMedModel{}.
The model matters most for \accentTopTrait{} (\accentTopShare{}) and length (\accentLengthShare{}, against \accentLengthPrompt{} for the prompt), and least for caveats (\accentCaveatShare{}).

\paragraph{Steady everywhere.}
The model's share is small but it does not wander (Table~\ref{tab:accent}).
The categories rank the models alike on em dashes ($W=\accentEmdashW$) and length ($W=\accentLengthW$), but not on caveats ($W=\accentCaveatW$) or warm openers ($W=\accentWarmW$).
From one run to the next, \accentRtHigh{} of \accentRtN{} traits keep the order of the six models at Spearman 0.89 or higher (median \accentRtMedian{}).
On the locked second prompt set, the pre-registered test held: \vTwoAccentPass{} accent traits kept the order of the first set (\vTwoAccentList{}), and the median model share was \vTwoMedModel{} (prompt \vTwoMedPrompt{}).
The least stable habits are again the generic ones: caveats keep their order at only \vTwoCaveatRt{}.

\paragraph{Faces and families.}
Each model's strongest habits are visible in Figure~\ref{fig:fingerprint}.
\shortModel{} writes \lenShort{} words on a typical answer and \longModel{} \lenLong{}; \shortModel{} is the shortest in \sweepShort{} categories and \longModel{} the longest in \sweepLong{}.
Some accents are a single habit: the card of \dashHiModel{} reads ``\cardFable''.
Siblings can split: \dashHiModel{} uses \dashHi{} em dashes per 100 words and \dashLoModel{}, from the same vendor, \dashLo{}.
Yet vendors do share something: models from the same vendor sit at a mean distance of \familySame{} in the family tree, against \familyDiff{} across vendors (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:family}).
The closest pair is \familyPair{}, on both prompt sets (distance \familyDist{} and \familyDistTwo{}).

\paragraph{Punctuation and words.}
The accent sits in the surface of the text.
A character classifier names the author of \abOriginal{} of single answers on unseen prompts; without markdown it still names \abNoMarkdown{}, without punctuation and symbols \abNoPunct{}, and with case and line breaks also removed \abBare{}.
Shuffling the remaining words costs only \abShuffleDrop{} more points (\abShuffled{}; \abShuffledMatched{} naming each model once per prompt): beyond punctuation, the accent is vocabulary, not word order.

\subsection{Models differ most in their reactions to prompts}
\label{sec:reactions}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{generated/fig_reactions.pdf}
\caption{What one answer is made of, with three answers per prompt (run \texttt{oct-23-26} answered twice more). For each style trait, the share of a single answer's variance that is the model's steady accent, its repeatable reactions to particular prompts, the prompt, and noise, from expected mean squares (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:reactions}).}
\label{fig:reactions}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The steady accent replicates.}
With three answers to every prompt, the model's median share of the variation is \repModel{}, as with one answer on the same prompts (\repModelOne; pre-registered).
What one answer could not separate now splits in two: a model's reaction to a particular prompt is significant against the noise between its repeat answers on all \repSig{} of \repNTraits{} style traits, and on all \repSigLater{} with only the two answers collected on the same day (pre-registered $F$ tests, every $p<10^{\repPExp}$).

\paragraph{Reactions outweigh the steady accent.}
Per answer, the model's steady accent is a median \vcModel{} of a trait's variance (\vcModelCI), its reactions \vcReact{} (\vcReactCI), the prompt \vcPrompt{} and noise \vcNoise{} (Figure~\ref{fig:reactions}; post hoc variance components; the registered sums of squares give \repModel{}, \ssReact{}, \ssPrompt{} and \ssNoise{}).
The reactions exceed the steady accent in every robustness check, by \reactRatioLow{} to \reactRatio{} times (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robust}; on ranks the accent is \robRanksModel{} and the reactions \robRanksReact{}), and together the two make up a median \vcModelReact{} of a trait's variance: which model wrote an answer shapes not only how much of a habit it has, but on which prompts the habit appears.
The extremes differ in kind.
Em dashes carry the largest steady accent: the model alone is \vcDashModel{} of an answer's variance, beside \vcDashReact{} for its reactions.
Length is set by the task: the prompt is \vcLengthPrompt{} of it and noise \vcLengthNoise.
The stereotyped habits are reactions: the model accounts for only \stereoModelLow{} to \stereoModelHigh{} of each, its reactions for \stereoReactLow{} to \stereoReactHigh{}; warm openers, for instance, are \vcWarmModel{} model and \vcWarmReact{} reaction.
Models differ little in how often they open warmly, but each does so on its own prompts.
This may be one reason the models' descriptions miss (Section~\ref{sec:hearing}): they describe how often a model does something, while models differ more in where they do it.

\paragraph{Some habits are mostly chance.}
The noisiest traits are \vcNoisiest{}: whether a given answer has them is largely a matter of sampling.
Models also differ in how much they repeat themselves: \consHighModel{} is the most self-consistent (\consHigh{} of its variation lies between prompts rather than between repeat answers) and \consLowModel{} the least (\consLow).

\label{sec:hearing}

\paragraph{They cannot describe them.}
Across \descSamples{} samples of \descNPerSample{} descriptions, the describers' estimates rank the panel above chance but weakly, at mean Spearman correlations of \descRankLow{} to \descRankHigh{}.
They describe the stereotype of AI writing: in the pre-registered sample, the median estimate overstates caveats \descCaveat{}-fold and warm openings \descWarm{}-fold, and understates how often models flag a pasted error (\descErrors{} times the measured rate); the prediction held in \descHFiveAll{} of four samples.
They know behavior better than style (rank \descBehaviorRank{} against \descStyleRank{}), and the habits that carry the accent least of all: the widest gap between siblings, \descEmGapMeasured{} standard deviations in em dashes, is seen as \descEmGapSeen{}.
In its estimates, no model knows itself better than others know it: across samples, a model's own placement differs from the others' placement of it by between \descSelfLow{} and \descSelfHigh{} places, in both directions.
The best guesser in the game describes itself worst: \texttt{gemini-4-argon} places itself \argonSelfPlaces{} places off on average, the others \argonOthersPlaces.

\paragraph{Their portraits barely help a reader.}
With the portraits the models wrote of themselves, \texttt{gemini-4-argon} names the others' answers \portraitSelf{} of the time, \portraitVsBlind{} relative to guessing blind (\portraitBlind) and \portraitVsMeasured{} relative to the measured profile (\portraitMeasured), on \portraitPrompts{} shared prompts (Figure~\ref{fig:hearing}, left).
Portraits written by other models give \portraitPeer{} (\portraitVsPeer{} for self-portraits against them, an exploratory comparison): a model's portrait of itself carries a little more of its voice than another model's portrait of it, the one sign of self-knowledge in these experiments, and both carry far less than the measurements.

\paragraph{They cannot imitate from a name.}
The pre-registered measure of imitation moved \imRegistered{} of the way toward the target, but most of that is not imitation: a fresh answer drifts from the single earlier answer it is measured from toward every other model's answer, and the same model asked to write as itself drifts \imDrift{} of the way.
Against that control, which we added after seeing the data, an imitation from the name moves \imName{} of the way (\imNameCI) (Figure~\ref{fig:hearing}, middle).
The drift behaves like regression to the mean: re-scoring the same imitations against the mean of three answers per prompt instead of one (pre-registered; no imitation was collected anew), it falls from \imDrift{} to \repDriftAvg{} (\repDriftAvgCI), about what regression to the mean predicts, and an imitation from the name still moves only \repNameAvg{} (\repNameAvgCI).
A classifier names the target \imNamedTarget{} of the time (\imNamedBase{} for self-imitations) and the imitator \imNamedImitator.
On traits an imitator had described on the wrong side, its imitations moved the right way \imIThree{} of the time, and its self-imitations \imIThreeSelf{}: imitation from a name shows no knowledge that the descriptions lack.

\paragraph{They cannot hide.}
Asked to rewrite their own answers so that no reader could tell which model wrote them, the models stay recognizable: a classifier that names \hideOriginal{} of the originals still names \hideUncoached{} (\hideUncoachedCI) of the rewrites (Figure~\ref{fig:hearing}, right; chance \chance).
Rewrites changed the accent traits more than the stereotyped ones (pre-registered difference \hideHTwo{}, \hideHTwoCI{}, against the predicted direction), although the stereotyped habits are rare, so most answers have little of them to change.

\subsection{Accents are contagious}
\label{sec:contagion}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{generated/fig_contagion.pdf}
\caption{Contagion, trait by trait: how far answers move toward the accent of the model whose notes were read, against answers after the reader's own notes, in standard deviations. Blue: the pre-registered accent traits; orange: the stereotyped ones. Whiskers: 95\% intervals over prompts.}
\label{fig:contagion}
\end{figure}

After reading five answers by another model as notes, a model's answers moved \conShift{} of the way toward that model's accent (\conShiftCI), against answers after its own notes; the pre-registered test held, and a classifier's probability for the notes' author rose by \conProb{} points (\conProbCI).
Trait by trait, the accent traits move more than the stereotyped ones (Figure~\ref{fig:contagion}): length (\conLength{} standard deviations), exclamations (\conExcl) and em dashes (\conDash) move toward the source, caveats (\conCaveat) and warm openers (\conWarm) do not; averaged, the pre-registered accent traits move \conAccent{} and the stereotyped ones \conStereo, exclamations being the one stereotyped habit that travels.
Reading its own notes does not amplify a model's accent: measured along the axis from the panel toward the reader's own accent, its answers move \conSelf{} of the way (\conSelfCI), slightly away from its accent.

\paragraph{Mostly content, partly voice.}
Another model's notes differ from one's own in what they say as well as in how they say it, and the style swap separates the two as far as rewriting can.
The rewrites changed the voice: a classifier named the rewriter of a rewritten note \swapRewriter{} of the time and its original author \swapAuthor{}, so the swap was real but partial.
Between note sets meant to carry the same content, notes in the source's voice moved answers \swapVoice{} of the way toward the source (\swapVoiceCI; \swapVoiceNoLen{} with length left out), and the classifier's probability for the source rose \swapProb{} points (\swapProbCI); all three pre-registered tests held.
Between note sets in the same voice, the source's content moved answers \swapContent{} of the way (\swapContentCI).
Most of what spreads, then, is what the notes say and the form that content takes; a smaller part, above zero in every registered test, appears to be the voice itself.
Rewriting kept the content only approximately, as we checked on names and numbers (post hoc): rewrites by another model kept \ccCrossKept{} of the original's, against \ccSelfKept{} when authors rewrote their own, and \ccCrossNovel{} of their names and numbers were new (\ccSelfNovel{} for self-rewrites).
A difference of this kind, common to all rewrites by another model, cancels in the voice effect; what would not cancel is a rewriter carrying in its own content, and the new names and numbers came from the rewriter's own answer to that prompt no more often than from any other model's (\ccImported{} against \ccControl, a difference of \ccExcess{} points, \ccExcessCI).
The check cannot see meaning, emphasis or recommendations, so these residual content differences limit a causal reading: the swap shows that voice can spread, more firmly than it fixes how much.
With every note rewritten, the original effect reappears: notes wholly the source's move answers \swapSSOO{} of the way, against notes wholly the reader's.

\paragraph{What a choice buys.}
A choice moves the average answer, not every answer: a model's steady accent is \vcModel{} of a single answer's variance and its reactions to particular prompts \vcReact{} (Section~\ref{sec:reactions}), so on a given prompt a usually short model can still answer at length.
How a model handles one particular prompt is a reaction mixed with sampling noise; judging it takes several answers.

\paragraph{Selection or steering.}
Switching models buys the whole accent, along with everything else that differs between the models.
Steering keeps the model: given another model's measured profile, a model moved \imProfile{} of the way toward that model's voice, against \imName{} from the name alone; asked to hide its own voice, its rewrites were still named \hideUncoached{} of the time, and \hideCoached{} once it was coached with its own measured habits.
Generic instructions do little, and asking a model how it writes returns the stereotype of AI writing, not its habits; measure them instead.
The accent is cheap to re-measure (\costRun{} in answers per run), so a fixed model name can be checked for a changed model behind it.

\paragraph{Shared notes and memory.}
Text an agent reads moves its writing toward the author's, mostly through what the text says and, it appears, partly through its voice, so in systems where agents share notes or memory, voices will blend unless that is managed.
Readers differ: \pEightMostReader{} moved furthest toward the notes it read and \pEightLeastReader{} showed little or no measured movement (Section~\ref{sec:eight}).
That suggests different sensitivity to supplied context, a hypothesis to test; it does not show which model uses memory better, which would require checking that useful facts are taken up and task quality kept.

\paragraph{Testing the recommendations.}
Whether choosing by measured profile helps is untested.
A pre-registered follow-up would route new prompts to the model whose profile matches a stated preference, compare it with a strong fixed model under the same instructions, and score preference fit and task quality separately, by people as well as by measurement; VibeCheck links measured traits to human preferences \citep{dunlap2025vibecheck}.

\section{Limitations and ethics}
\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Panel and prompts.} Six models from four providers (eight from six in the generalization test), synthetic English prompts written by the authors, one answer per prompt per run (three in one collection, for the style traits only), and no system prompt; effect sizes are relative to this panel, and habits may change under a product's instructions.
\paragraph{A confound in the imitation test.} The pre-registered imitation metric measures each imitation from a single earlier answer, which any fresh answer drifts away from; we report the registered values and a control added after seeing the data, and the claims about imitation rest on the control. Re-scoring the same imitations against three-answer baselines (pre-registered) supports regression to the mean as the cause, but the corrected comparison has not been replicated with new imitations.
\paragraph{Reactions.} The reactions are estimated on one prompt set and one run, with two of the three answers collected \repDaysLater{} days after the first; whether a model's reaction to a given prompt survives a model update is untested.
\paragraph{Measurement by machines.} Traits are computed by code and behaviors by two coder models, which can match crowd annotators \citep{gilardi2023chatgpt} but are not validated here against human judgment; recognition and hiding are scored by a classifier and one guesser. Whether people hear the same accents is untested.
\paragraph{Single exposures.} Contagion was measured after one exposure to five notes; whether it accumulates over many exposures, as in long-running agents with shared memory, is the natural next test. The style swap keeps content only as well as rewriting does: a partial change of voice would weaken the voice effect, a content loss common to all rewrites by another model cancels, and content a rewriter carries in from its own answers would inflate it; our check covers names and numbers, not meaning, emphasis or recommendations.
\paragraph{Time.} Two runs \daysApart{} days apart and one locked prompt set show stability over days, not across model versions, and model behavior is known to drift between versions \citep{chen2024behaviorchange} and to vary between identical runs \citep{atil2024stability}.
\paragraph{Ethics.} Identifying the model behind an answer has legitimate uses, such as auditing which model a vendor serves, and less welcome ones; our profiles describe public models' public behavior, and prior work identifies models more accurately by other means \citep{sun2025idiosyncrasies,pasquini2025llmmap}. The hiding experiment shows that measured habits help a model disguise its output, which bears on detection; we release it because the finding that generic instructions fail matters to anyone relying on them.

\section{Conclusion}
Frontier models write with accents: a steady part, small in any single answer but constant across tasks, runs and new prompts and carried by punctuation and word choice, and a larger part made of each model's own reactions to particular prompts.
The models mishear them: they describe the stereotype of AI writing, their portraits of themselves help a reader only a little, and they cannot imitate each other from a name or hide.
Measurement makes the accent usable, as a description that transfers, a voice to imitate, or habits to drop, and part of the accent travels with the text to whoever reads it, more to some readers than to others.
The accent, its misheard descriptions and contagion held when two models from two more vendors joined the panel.

\section{Variance components}
\label{app:variance}
For one style trait, let $y_{mps}$ be answer $s$ of model $m$ to prompt $p$, with $M$ models, $P$ prompts on which every model has all $S=3$ answers, and means $\bar y_{m}$, $\bar y_{p}$, $\bar y_{mp}$ and $\bar y$.
The two-way analysis of variance with replication splits the total sum of squares into
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{SS}_M &= PS\textstyle\sum_m(\bar y_m-\bar y)^2, &
\mathrm{SS}_P &= MS\textstyle\sum_p(\bar y_p-\bar y)^2,\\
\mathrm{SS}_{MP} &= S\textstyle\sum_{m,p}(\bar y_{mp}-\bar y_m-\bar y_p+\bar y)^2, &
\mathrm{SS}_E &= \textstyle\sum_{m,p,s}(y_{mps}-\bar y_{mp})^2,
\end{align*}
with mean squares $\mathrm{MS}=\mathrm{SS}/\mathrm{df}$ on $M-1$, $P-1$, $(M-1)(P-1)$ and $MP(S-1)$ degrees of freedom.
The pre-registered quantities are the shares $\mathrm{SS}/\mathrm{SS}_\text{total}$ and the test $F=\mathrm{MS}_{MP}/\mathrm{MS}_E$ of the model's reactions against noise.
Because $\operatorname{E}[\mathrm{MS}_{MP}]=\sigma^2_E+S\sigma^2_{MP}$, the interaction's sum of squares carries part of the noise; the post hoc variance components remove it.
Treating models, prompts and their interaction as random effects, the expected mean squares give
\[
\hat\sigma^2_E=\mathrm{MS}_E,\quad
\hat\sigma^2_{MP}=\frac{\mathrm{MS}_{MP}-\mathrm{MS}_E}{S},\quad
\hat\sigma^2_M=\frac{\mathrm{MS}_M-\mathrm{MS}_{MP}}{PS},\quad
\hat\sigma^2_P=\frac{\mathrm{MS}_P-\mathrm{MS}_{MP}}{MS},
\]
negative estimates set to zero, and each part's share of one answer's variance is its estimate over their sum.
Intervals come from \bootReps{} resamples of prompts, one draw shared by all traits since they are measured on the same answers; medians are taken over traits within each resample.
Three robustness checks repeat the estimate on the two answers collected on the same day, on everyday prompts only (no trap, no stated limit), and on ranks of the trait values instead of the values (Table~\ref{tab:robust}).
In every check the reactions exceed the steady accent.
