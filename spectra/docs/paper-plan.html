Spectra Paper Plan

Related work and roadmap · as of 24 Oct 2026

A snapshot of the shared doc for co-authors. The live, editable version is here; comment and edit there, not in this file.

Purpose and status

We propose Spectra as a standalone paper: arXiv first, then an ACL Rolling Review cycle in early 2027. Its central result: models name each other's unsigned answers 40.1% of the time blind (chance 16.7%) and 80.9% with notes measured on other prompts, nearly the 81.8% that notes measured on the answers themselves reach.

  • Positioning: Spectra is a standalone paper. It cites PeerRank as one related work, not as a companion: arXiv 2602.02589, whose v2 (20 Oct 2026) is retitled Agents-as-Peers: Bias-Controlled LLM Evaluation as Multi-Agent Peer Assessment and adds Seffi Cohen as an author. Cite that title.
  • Data in hand: two runs of the same 200 prompts (oct-14-26, oct-23-26) across 6 models: 1,200 answers and 2,400 coded answers per run, plus 4,768 guessing calls in four games on oct-23-26.
  • Related work: each paper below was checked against its arXiv, venue or abstract page on 24 Oct 2026. Two items are flagged where a detail is unconfirmed.

Related work: telling models apart

Chat models can be told apart with high accuracy, but prior work does it with opaque classifiers or probing queries, not with named habits.

Paper Venue Key finding Why it matters for Spectra
Idiosyncrasies in Large Language Models (Sun, Yin, Xu, Kolter, Liu) ICML 2025 A fine-tuned embedding model names the source among ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini and DeepSeek with 97.1% accuracy. The signal sits in word choice and survives rewriting, translation and summarizing. Closest prior work. Its classifier is opaque; Spectra measures traits a reader can name.
VibeCheck (Dunlap, Mandal, Darrell, Steinhardt, Gonzalez) ICLR 2025 Discovers "vibes" such as tone and formatting and scores them with a panel of LLM judges. For Llama-3-70b vs GPT-4 they predicted the author 80% of the time and user preference 61%. Pairwise and judge-based. Spectra profiles six models at once and computes style without a judge.
Authorship Attribution for Neural Text Generation (Uchendu, Le, Shu, Lee) and TuringBench EMNLP 2020; Findings of EMNLP 2021 Defined the task of naming which of k generators wrote a text. Stronger generators confused the classifiers more. The pre-chat version of the task.
LLMmap (Pasquini, Kornaropoulos, Ateniese) USENIX Security 2025 About 8 crafted queries identify 42 model versions with over 95% accuracy, even behind unknown system prompts. Active probing, where Spectra observes answers. Its misuse angle belongs in our ethics section.
Hide and Seek (Iourovitski, Sharma, Talwar) arXiv 2024 An auditor model writes probing prompts and a detective model names the model family, correct 72% of the time. An early case of models identifying other models.
Who's Behind the Text? (Ko et al.) arXiv, 14 Oct 2026 ATLAS attributes text by its likelihood under each source. It reaches 74.27% when domain and model version both shift, 9.00 points above the best baseline, and recovers distillation teachers. Attribution degrades under shift, which motivates Spectra's held-out test.
Authorship Attribution in the Era of LLMs (Huang, Chen, Shu) arXiv 2024, survey Maps the attribution problem space for LLM text. For framing the field.

Related work: models recognizing themselves and each other

Evidence for genuine self-recognition is weak; style, length and familiarity explain much of what looks like it.

Paper Venue Key finding Why it matters for Spectra
LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations (Panickssery, Bowman, Feng) NeurIPS 2024 Models tell their own text from others' better than chance. After fine-tuning, self-recognition and self-preference rise together, linearly. Links Spectra's "found own answer" score to self-preference in model judges.
Self-Recognition in Language Models (Davidson et al.) Findings of EMNLP 2024 No consistent self-recognition: models pick the "best" answer, not their own, and agree on which answers are best. Matches Spectra's uneven self-recognition: 88% for gpt-6-astra, 16% for muse-wonder-1.0.
Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge (Wataoka, Takahashi, Ri) arXiv 2024 Judges rate lower-perplexity text higher, whoever wrote it, so familiarity drives self-preference. A mechanism to test against Spectra's style distances.
Style, Not Self (Barkhordar, Thapa) arXiv, 24 Sep 2026 Self-recognition of code sits near chance in balanced accuracy (49–58%). Pairwise accuracy tracks which answer is longer (r = 0.93), and 10 of 12 results fall to chance once style is stripped. Closest new paper. Spectra asks the same question of prose, with named cues and research notes as an extra condition.
Great Models Think Alike (Goel et al.) ICML 2025 Judges favor models similar to themselves, and stronger models increasingly share mistakes. Spectra's "closest neighbor" and same-vendor differences test this from the style side.

Related work: style skewing evaluation

Length and formatting move evaluations, so measuring them directly helps explain ranking results.

Paper Venue Key finding Why it matters for Spectra
Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al.) NeurIPS 2023 Named the position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases of model judges. With controls, a GPT-4 judge agreed with humans over 80% of the time. The bias vocabulary Spectra's "no judge" design answers.
Length-Controlled AlpacaEval (Dubois, Galambosi, Liang, Hashimoto) arXiv 2024 Regressing out length raised AlpacaEval's Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98. Length is the loudest habit in Spectra too.
Style control (LMSYS) Blog, Aug 2024 Controlling for length and markdown counts reshuffled the Arena leaderboard. Shows style moves human preference, not only model judges.
YapBench (Borisov, Gröger, Mikhael, Schreiber) arXiv, Jan 2026 Across 76 assistant models, median excess length varies by about an order of magnitude. Models pad ambiguous inputs and over-format one-line coding answers. Measures verbosity alone; Spectra measures 23 traits together.
Agents-as-Peers (Margalit, Cohen-Inger, Avram, Taig, Cohen, Margalit) arXiv 2026, v2 12 LLMs, 420 questions, 55,440 non-self judgments per regime. Self, identity and position effects (+0.39 in the first slot); peer scores track TruthfulQA (r = 0.904) and GSM8K (r = 0.873). Its abstract does not analyze style. Spectra measures that side directly.

Related work: the behaviors Spectra's traps test

Each Spectra trap has a dedicated benchmark; Spectra hides the same tests inside everyday prompts instead of a test set.

Paper Venue Key finding Spectra trap or trait
Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models (Sharma et al.) ICLR 2024 Five assistants agreed with users' views and repeated their mistakes. Human preference data rewards agreeable answers. Stated opinion (OP), pasted error (ERR)
Won't Get Fooled Again (Hu et al.) ACL 2023 FalseQA: 2,365 false-premise questions. About 256 fine-tuning examples teach models to spot and rebut them. False premise (FP)
CLAMBER (Zhang et al.) ACL 2024 12K examples. Models are poor at spotting ambiguity and asking good clarifying questions; chain-of-thought and few-shot help only marginally. Vague request (AMB)
Relying on the Unreliable (Zhou, Hwang, Ren, Sap) ACL 2024 Models rarely express uncertainty; 47% of their confident answers are wrong. Preference data penalizes hedging. Hedging trait
IFEval (Zhou et al.) arXiv 2023 25 kinds of instructions a program can check, across about 500 prompts. The 27 prompts with an explicit limit

Related work: vocabulary markers and measurement

Spectra's text measures, replication and reliability checks rest on established methods; two citations below still need confirming.

Paper Venue Key finding Why it matters for Spectra
Delving into ChatGPT usage in academic writing through excess vocabulary (Kobak, González-Márquez, Horvát, Lause) arXiv 2024 Style words such as "delves" surged in 14 million PubMed abstracts; at least 10% of 2024 abstracts were processed with an LLM. Grounds the stock-phrases trait. To confirm: a press report says it appeared in Science Advances; we did not find the DOI.
Measuring AI "Slop" in Text (Shaib, Chakrabarty, Garcia-Olano, Wallace) arXiv 2025 Interpretable dimensions for low-quality AI text, from expert interviews; binary "slop" judgments are partly subjective. Vocabulary for naming style traits.
How Is ChatGPT's Behavior Changing over Time? (Chen, Zaharia, Zou) Harvard Data Science Review 2024 GPT-4's accuracy on prime numbers fell from 84% to 51% between its March and June 2023 versions. Motivates Spectra's two-run replication.
LLM Stability (Atil et al.) arXiv 2024 Six models, eight tasks, five identical runs at deterministic settings: accuracy varied by up to 10%. Run-to-run noise to report beside Spectra's stability results.
ChatGPT outperforms crowd workers for text-annotation tasks (Gilardi, Alizadeh, Kubli) PNAS 2023 ChatGPT's intercoder agreement exceeded crowd workers' and trained annotators' on four datasets. Precedent for model coders; Spectra reports agreement per label.
Fightin' Words (Monroe, Colaresi, Quinn) Political Analysis 2008 Log-odds ratios with an informative Dirichlet prior for words that separate groups. The method behind Spectra's signature words.
Moving-average type-token ratio (Covington, McFall) Journal of Quantitative Linguistics 2010 A vocabulary-range measure less sensitive to text length. The vocabulary-range trait. To confirm: cited from memory; our search did not find the paper.

The gap Spectra fills

No paper we found combines these six elements; Spectra does, for one deployment, measured twice.

  • Style measured without a judge: 13 traits computed from the text, from length and formatting to hedging, em dashes and reading ease.
  • Behavior labels with reported reliability: 10 labels per answer from two coder models outside the panel, with Cohen's kappa per label.
  • Traps inside ordinary prompts: 43 of the 200 prompts hide one of five traps, and 27 state a length or format limit that a program checks.
  • Replication: the same 200 prompts, run twice, 9 days apart.
  • Models identifying each other: blind, with notes from the same answers, and with notes from other prompts; each model used once, or each answer named on its own.
  • Every claim computed from the run: usage recommendations and report text are derived from the measurements, and tests fail if a finding from an old run is pasted in as fixed text.

Paper plan: title, thesis, contributions

Working title: Spectra: Judge-Free Behavioral Fingerprints of Frontier LLMs, and What Models Know About Each Other. Shorter alternative: Can Models Tell Each Other Apart?

Thesis: frontier models have stable, nameable writing habits. They can be measured without a judge, they repeat across runs, models can name each other from them, and a plain written description of them works on prompts it was never measured on.

Contributions:

  1. Measurement without a judge: 13 computed style traits, 10 coded behaviors with per-label agreement, traps hidden in ordinary prompts, program-checked limits, and effect sizes against the panel with intervals that resample prompts.
  2. A peer identification game in three conditions (blind, notes from the same answers, notes from other prompts), under two naming rules, with own answers scored apart, a majority vote, a classifier scored the same way, and a fixed-effects analysis of prompts, guessers and authors.
  3. Replication: two runs of the same 200 prompts, 9 days apart.
  4. Recommendations from measured traits, with no judge, and tests that catch a stale finding pasted in as fixed text.
  5. An open release: the pipeline, the answers, the coder labels and the guesses.

Claims and current evidence

The headline claim is the transfer: habits measured on one half of the prompts identify authors on the other half almost as well as notes measured on the answers themselves.

Notes from other prompts keep 98% of the gain that notes add over guessing blind, under the same naming rule as the same-answer game (each model used once). Naming each answer on its own changes little: 79.5%, with a name repeated in 4% of calls. Scored the way a guesser plays, all six answers to a prompt at once and each model once, the classifier trained on the other half of the prompts names 74.7% (78.1% when trained on four fifths of them): one guesser with held-out notes is level with it or above, and the vote is clearly above. Chance is 1 in 6 throughout. The majority-vote rows give each answer to the model most of the other five guessers named, ties split evenly, with no model voting on its own answer: they measure how identifiable the models are, not what one guesser knows.

Why it matters

The paper turns model fingerprints from a classifier's output into habits people can name, check and act on.

Scientifically:

  • It shows identifying cues can be written down in plain words and still work on unseen prompts.
  • It separates style from self-knowledge with interpretable cues, the test Style, Not Self calls for, extended from code to prose.
  • It offers a measured, stylistic account of the self-preference and identity effects reported for model judges.
  • It separates prompt difficulty from guesser skill: with notes, which prompt is answered explains about four times as much of the variance in success as which model guesses.

Practically:

  • Choose a model by its habits, not only its rank: length, answering first, flagging errors.
  • Prompt each model for its known weak spots, and pair models whose misses do not overlap.
  • Watch for behavior drift by rerunning the same prompts.
  • Know that a model behind a product can be identified from its writing alone.

Paper outline

Eight to nine pages plus an appendix, in this order:

  1. Introduction: the gap in how models are compared, the thesis, the contributions, and three headline numbers: 40.1% blind, 81.8% with notes from the same answers, 80.9% with notes from other prompts.
  2. Related work: the four threads above, attribution, self-recognition, style in evaluation, and the trap behaviors.
  3. Spectra: prompts, traps and limits; how models are run; the two measurement layers; the statistics; the guessing game.
  4. Setup: the two runs, the six models, the two coders, and costs ($20.56 per collection run, $59 to $97 per game).
  5. Results:
    1. Profiles and stability across runs
    2. Traps
    3. The author classifier
    4. The guessing game: three note conditions, two naming rules, the classifier scored the same way, prompt difficulty, self-recognition
    5. Reliability: coder agreement, the sensitivity check, and the third-coder tie-break
  6. Practical implications: model choice, prompting, pairing, drift monitoring.
  7. Limitations and ethics: synthetic English prompts, effect sizes relative to this panel, model coders, API drift, and fingerprinting models behind products.
  8. Appendix: the label list, the prompt set, trait formulas, the p020 walkthrough, and reproduction steps.

Publication route

Post to arXiv about six weeks from now, then submit to an ACL Rolling Review cycle in early 2027.

Venue deadlines here are typical windows, not confirmed 2027 dates; check each venue's site. ICLR 2027's deadline has most likely already passed for this cycle, and ACL or NeurIPS 2027 workshops are a cheap early signal.
